# Trusted Setup
Groth16 proofs are built on **pairing-based cryptography**. The prover needs to commit to polynomials evaluated at a secret point $\tau$ (tau), but if $\tau$ were known, a cheating prover could forge proofs. The setup encodes powers of $\tau$ into elliptic curve group elements — making the evaluations usable while keeping $\tau$ itself hidden (assuming the discrete log is hard).
 
The toxic waste consists of the raw scalars:
 
$$\tau, \alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta \in \mathbb{F}_r$$
 
where $\mathbb{F}_r$ is the scalar field of the chosen curve. **If any of these values leak, the system is broken.**

Before the setup can run, the circuit must be compiled into a Rank-1 Constraint system(R1CS), the converted to a Quadratic Arithmetic Program (QAP)

**R1CS-> QAP**

Given  M wires and n contraints (where a single wire considered a single variable going into the system), the QAP encodes the constraints as polynomial identities. For witness $\mathbf{w} = (1, \text{pub}_1, \ldots, \text{pub}_\ell, \text{priv}_{\ell+1}, \ldots, \text{priv}_m)$:

$$\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot u_i(x)\right) \cdot \left(\sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot v_i(x)\right) = \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot w_i(x) + h(x) \cdot t(x)$$

where:
- $u_i(x), v_i(x), w_i(x)$ are the **QAP polynomials** (one set per wire)
- $t(x) = (x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n)$ is the **vanishing polynomial** over $n$ constraint roots
- $h(x)$ is the **quotient polynomial** computed by the prover

The QAP polynomials are fixed by the circuit. The setup encodes them at $\tau$.

## Groth16 Setup Phase 1 and Phase 2

Modern Groth16 setups are split into two phases.

### Phase 1: Powers of Tau (Circuit Agnostic)
Phase 1 generates generic powers of Tau in both $\mathbb{G}_1$ and $\mathbb{G}_2$ (The Group of the pairing). These do **not** depend on any circuit and can be reused across many circuits up to  maximum of degree.

The output is:
 
$$\left[\tau^0\right]_1, \left[\tau^1\right]_1, \ldots, \left[\tau^{n-1}\right]_1 \in \mathbb{G}_1$$
$$\left[\tau^0\right]_2, \left[\tau^1\right]_2, \ldots, \left[\tau^{n-1}\right]_2 \in \mathbb{G}_2$$

where the bracket notation $[x]_k$ means $x \cdot G_k$ (scalar multiplication by the generator of group $k$).

If you wanna learn more about this, you can Study KZG commiments for it. 😃

### Phase 2: Circuit Specific Setup (Key generation)
Given the QAP polynomials from the compiled circuit and the Phase 1 output, phase 2 samples the addition toxic waste $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ and computes the final **Proving Key (PK)** and **Verification Key (VK)**.
#### Toxic Waste
 
$$\tau, \alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_r$$
These must be **independently sampled and destroyed**.


## The Proving Key (PK)
The proving Key contains all the group elements the prover needs to construct a proof.

### $\mathbb{G}_1$ elements in the PK:

$$\alpha \cdot G_1, \quad \delta \cdot G_1$$
 
$$\left[\tau^i\right]_1 \quad \text{for } i = 0, \ldots, n-2 \quad \text{(for computing } h(\tau) \cdot t(\tau)\text{)}$$
 
$$\left[\frac{\beta \cdot u_i(\tau) + \alpha \cdot v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1 \quad \text{for } i = \ell+1, \ldots, m \quad \text{(private wire encodings)}$$
 
### $\mathbb{G}_2$ elements in the PK:

$$\beta \cdot G_2, \quad \delta \cdot G_2$$
 
$$\left[\tau^i\right]_2 \quad \text{for } i = 0, \ldots, n-2$$

The quotient contribution $\left[\frac{\tau^i \cdot t(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$ for $i = 0, \ldots, n-2$ is also included to allow the prover to commit to $h(\tau) \cdot t(\tau) / \delta$ without knowing $\tau$ or $\delta$.

## The Verification Key (VK)
 
The verification key is small and fixed. It contains only what the verifier needs to check the three pairing equations.
 
$$[\alpha]_1, \quad [\beta]_2, \quad [\gamma]_2, \quad [\delta]_2$$
 
$$\left[\frac{\beta \cdot u_i(\tau) + \alpha \cdot v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\gamma}\right]_1 \quad \text{for } i = 0, \ldots, \ell \quad \text{(public wire encodings)}$$
 
The separation by $\gamma$ (for public inputs) vs $\delta$ (for private inputs) is a key Groth16 design choice — it prevents the prover from mixing public and private commitments.
 
 # Groth16 Proof Structure
 A groth16 proof consists of just three group element

 $$\pi = (A, B, C) \in \mathbb{G}_1 \times \mathbb{G}_2 \times \mathbb{G}_1$$
 
 ### Prover's computation (using PK):
 
Sample blinding factors $r, s \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_r$, then:
 
$$A = \alpha \cdot G_1 + \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot [u_i(\tau)]_1 + r \cdot \delta \cdot G_1$$
 
$$B = \beta \cdot G_2 + \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot [v_i(\tau)]_2 + s \cdot \delta \cdot G_2$$
 
$$C = \frac{\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m} w_i \cdot (\beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau))}{\delta} \cdot G_1 + h(\tau) \cdot \frac{t(\tau)}{\delta} \cdot G_1 + s \cdot A + r \cdot B - r \cdot s \cdot \delta \cdot G_1$$

## Verification: The Pairing Equations
 
The verifier checks:
 
$$e(A, B) = e(\alpha \cdot G_1, \beta \cdot G_2) \cdot e\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} w_i \cdot K_i, \gamma \cdot G_2\right) \cdot e(C, \delta \cdot G_2)$$
 
where $K_i = \frac{\beta \cdot u_i(\tau) + \alpha \cdot v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\gamma} \cdot G_1$ from the VK.
 
This single equation (computable with 3 pairings and 1 multi-scalar multiplication) simultaneously verifies:
1. The QAP relation holds at $\tau$
2. The prover used a consistent witness
3. The $h(x) \cdot t(x)$ factorisation is valid

# Proving Mechanism
Given a compiled circuit, a valid witness, and the proving Key produced by the trusted setup, the groth16 prover constrcuts a succinct proof, $\pi = (A, B, C)$ — three elliptic curve group elements — that convinces a verifier the prover knows a valid witness without revealing it.

Step 0:
The prover recieves 
1. The Proving Key: 
Out of the trusted setup 

2. **The public inputs** $\mathbf{x} = (w_1, \ldots, w_\ell) \in \mathbb{F}_r^\ell$

3. **The private witness** $\mathbf{w}_{\text{priv}} = (w_{\ell+1}, \ldots, w_m) \in \mathbb{F}_r^{m-\ell}$

Together, the full witness vector is:
 
$$\mathbf{w} = (1,\ w_1,\ w_2,\ \ldots,\ w_\ell,\ w_{\ell+1},\ \ldots,\ w_m)$$
 
The $w_0 = 1$ slot is the constant wire used to encode constant terms in constraints.

Its usually always 1 to make the calculations Easier.

## Step 1: Witness Extension — Satisfying the R1CS

Before any polynomial arithmetic, the prover must verify (and extend) the witness so that **all R1CS constraints are satisfied**:

We apply the Multilinear Extension

$$\forall j \in [n]: \quad \langle \mathbf{a}_j, \mathbf{w} \rangle \cdot \langle \mathbf{b}_j, \mathbf{w} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{c}_j, \mathbf{w} \rangle$$

where $\mathbf{a}_j, \mathbf{b}_j, \mathbf{c}_j \in \mathbb{F}_r^{m+1}$ are the constraint coefficient vectors for constraint $j$.

In circuits with sub-components (e.g., hash gadgets, range checks), this step includes computing intermediate wire values via **witness generation** — evaluating every gate in topological order to fill in the full $\mathbf{w}$.
 
> **If any constraint fails here, the prover is stuck — no valid proof exists for this witness.**
 
In `ark-groth16`, this is handled by the `R1CS` constraint system's `is_satisfied()` check and the witness map from the circuit's `ConstraintSynthesizer


## Step 2: QAP Polynomial Construction
 
The R1CS is encoded as a **Quadratic Arithmetic Program (QAP)**. The prover constructs three linear combinations of polynomials:

$$U(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot u_i(x), \qquad V(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot v_i(x), \qquad W(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot w_i(x)$$
 
where $u_i, v_i, w_i \in \mathbb{F}_r^{<n}[x]$ are the QAP polynomials (degree $< n$), each satisfying:
 
$$u_i(r_j) = a_{j,i}, \quad v_i(r_j) = b_{j,i}, \quad w_i(r_j) = c_{j,i}$$
 
for constraint root $r_j$ (the $j$-th root of the vanishing polynomial $t(x)$).
 
Because R1CS is satisfied, the polynomial identity holds at every constraint root:
 
$$U(r_j) \cdot V(r_j) = W(r_j) \quad \forall j \in [n]$$

## Step 3: Computing the Quotient Polynomial $h(x)$
 
The QAP identity
 
$$U(x) \cdot V(x) - W(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{t(x)}$$
 
means $t(x)$ divides $U(x) \cdot V(x) - W(x)$ exactly. The prover computes:
 
$$h(x) = \frac{U(x) \cdot V(x) - W(x)}{t(x)}$$
 
where $t(x) = \prod_{j=1}^{n}(x - r_j)$ has degree $n$.
 
Since $U, V$ have degree $< n$, their product has degree $< 2n$, and $W$ has degree $< n$. Thus:
 
$$\deg(h) < n - 1$$
 
### Efficient Computation via FFT
 
The naive approach (polynomial multiplication then long division) is $O(n^2)$. In practice:
 
1. Evaluate $U, V, W$ at the $2n$ roots of unity $\omega^0, \ldots, \omega^{2n-1}$ using **FFT** over the evaluation domain $H$.
2. Compute $P(\omega^i) = U(\omega^i) \cdot V(\omega^i) - W(\omega^i)$ pointwise.
3. Divide by $t(\omega^i)$ pointwise (valid since $t$ does not vanish on the extended domain).
4. Interpolate back to get $h(x)$ using **IFFT**.
 
Total cost: $O(n \log n)$.
 
In `ark-groth16`:
```
// Simplified sketch
let u_evals = fft(u_coeffs, domain_2n);
let v_evals = fft(v_coeffs, domain_2n);
let w_evals = fft(w_coeffs, domain_2n);
let h_evals: Vec<_> = u_evals.iter().zip(v_evals).zip(w_evals)
    .map(|((u, v), w)| (u * v - w) * t_inv)
    .collect();
let h_coeffs = ifft(h_evals, domain_2n);
```

 ## Step 4: Sampling Blinding Factors
 
To achieve **zero-knowledge** — ensuring the proof reveals nothing about the private witness — the prover samples two fresh random scalars:
 
$$r, s \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_r$$
 
These are used to rerandomize the proof elements. Without them, the proof would be a **Proof of Knowledge** but **not** zero-knowledge (the Groth16 paper proves the scheme is both).
 
---
 
## Step 5: Computing Proof Element $A \in \mathbb{G}_1$
 
$$A = [\alpha]_1 + \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot [u_i(\tau)]_1 + r \cdot [\delta]_1$$
 
Breaking this down:
 
| Term | Source | Role |
|---|---|---|
| $[\alpha]_1$ | PK directly | Anchors $A$ to the setup's $\alpha$ |
| $\sum w_i \cdot [u_i(\tau)]_1$ | MSM over PK elements | Commits to the left QAP input $U(\tau)$ |
| $r \cdot [\delta]_1$ | PK + random $r$ | Rerandomization for ZK |
 
The middle term is a **Multi-Scalar Multiplication (MSM)**: given $m+1$ group elements and $m+1$ scalars, compute their linear combination. This is the dominant cost in proof generation — $O(m / \log m)$ group operations via Pippenger's algorithm.
 
---
 
## Step 6: Computing Proof Element $B \in \mathbb{G}_2$
 
$$B = [\beta]_2 + \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot [v_i(\tau)]_2 + s \cdot [\delta]_2$$
 
This is structurally identical to $A$ but lives in $\mathbb{G}_2$ and uses the $v_i$ polynomials:
 
| Term | Source | Role |
|---|---|---|
| $[\beta]_2$ | PK directly | Anchors $B$ to setup's $\beta$ |
| $\sum w_i \cdot [v_i(\tau)]_2$ | MSM over $\mathbb{G}_2$ PK elements | Commits to right QAP input $V(\tau)$ |
| $s \cdot [\delta]_2$ | PK + random $s$ | Rerandomization for ZK |
 
> **Note:** $\mathbb{G}_2$ MSMs are significantly more expensive than $\mathbb{G}_1$ MSMs due to the larger field arithmetic in $\mathbb{G}_2$. This is typically the second-most expensive step.
 
There is also a $\mathbb{G}_1$ version of $B$ computed internally:
 
$$B_1 = [\beta]_1 + \sum_{i=0}^{m} w_i \cdot [v_i(\tau)]_1 + s \cdot [\delta]_1$$
 
This is used in computing $C$ below.
 
---
 
## Step 7: Computing Proof Element $C \in \mathbb{G}_1$
 
$C$ is the most complex element. It commits to the private witness, the quotient polynomial, and the rerandomization cross-terms:
 
$$C = \underbrace{\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m} w_i \cdot \left[\frac{\beta \cdot u_i(\tau) + \alpha \cdot v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1}_{\text{private witness commitment}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{n-2} h_i \cdot \left[\frac{\tau^i \cdot t(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1}_{\text{quotient polynomial commitment}} + \underbrace{s \cdot A + r \cdot B_1 - r \cdot s \cdot [\delta]_1}_{\text{ZK rerandomization}}$$
 
### Term-by-term breakdown:
 
**1. Private witness commitment:**
 
$$\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m} w_i \cdot \left[\frac{\beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$$
 
These PK elements (one per private wire) encode the "cross-product" combination of all three QAP polynomials, divided by $\delta$. The $\delta$ in the denominator means only the prover — who knows the PK but not $\delta$ — can form this term. It is the **core soundness mechanism**: a cheating prover cannot forge $C$ without knowing valid witness scalars $w_i$.
 
**2. Quotient polynomial commitment:**
 
$$\sum_{i=0}^{n-2} h_i \cdot \left[\frac{\tau^i \cdot t(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$$
 
Here $h_i$ are the coefficients of $h(x)$ computed in Step 3. The PK elements $\left[\frac{\tau^i \cdot t(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$ allow committing to $h(\tau) \cdot t(\tau) / \delta$ without knowing $\tau$ or $\delta$. This ensures the verifier can check that $U(\tau) \cdot V(\tau) - W(\tau) = h(\tau) \cdot t(\tau)$.
 
**3. ZK rerandomization:**
 
$$s \cdot A + r \cdot B_1 - r \cdot s \cdot [\delta]_1$$
 
This uses the blinding factors $r$ and $s$ introduced in Step 4 to mask all three proof elements simultaneously. The cross-terms ensure that the pairing equation still holds after rerandomization:
 
$$e(A, B) = e(\alpha, \beta) \cdot e(\text{pub}, \gamma) \cdot e(C, \delta)$$
 
remains satisfied because the $r, s$ terms cancel algebraically across the three pairing slots.
 
---
 
## Full Proof Formula — Summary
 
$$\boxed{A = [\alpha]_1 + U(\tau)_1 + r[\delta]_1}$$
 
$$\boxed{B = [\beta]_2 + V(\tau)_2 + s[\delta]_2}$$
 
$$\boxed{C = \frac{\sum_{\text{priv}} w_i K_i}{\delta}\bigg|_1 + \frac{h(\tau) t(\tau)}{\delta}\bigg|_1 + sA + rB_1 - rs[\delta]_1}$$
 
where $K_i = \beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)$ are the cross-product wire encodings.

## Computational Cost Profile
 
| Step | Operation | Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Witness extension | Gate evaluation | $O(m)$ field ops |
| QAP polynomials $U, V, W$ | IFFT over $n$ roots | $O(n \log n)$ |
| Quotient $h(x)$ | FFT multiply + divide | $O(n \log n)$ |
| $A$: MSM in $\mathbb{G}_1$ | $m+1$ scalars | $O(m / \log m)$ $\mathbb{G}_1$ ops |
| $B$: MSM in $\mathbb{G}_2$ | $m+1$ scalars | $O(m / \log m)$ $\mathbb{G}_2$ ops |
| $C$: MSM in $\mathbb{G}_1$ | $(m - \ell) + (n-1)$ scalars | $O(m / \log m)$ $\mathbb{G}_1$ ops |
 
The **MSMs dominate** for large circuits. $\mathbb{G}_2$ arithmetic is typically $3\text{–}5\times$ slower than $\mathbb{G}_1$ on BN254/BLS12-381. For circuits with millions of constraints, GPU-accelerated MSMs (e.g., using `icicle`, `bellman-cuda`) reduce proving time from minutes to seconds.

## Why the Pairing Equation Still Holds
 
The verifier checks:
 
$$e(A, B) \stackrel{?}{=} e([\alpha]_1, [\beta]_2) \cdot e\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} w_i K_i^{\text{pub}}, [\gamma]_2\right) \cdot e(C, [\delta]_2)$$
 
Substituting the honest prover's $A, B, C$ (with $r = s = 0$ for clarity):
 
$$e\!\left([\alpha + U(\tau)]_1,\ [\beta + V(\tau)]_2\right)$$
$$= e([\alpha], [\beta]) \cdot e([\alpha], [V(\tau)]_2) \cdot e([U(\tau)]_1, [\beta]) \cdot e([U(\tau)]_1, [V(\tau)]_2)$$
 
The $e([U]_1, [V]_2)$ term equals $e([W(\tau) + h(\tau)t(\tau)]_1, [1]_2)$ by QAP satisfaction. This splits into the public and private wire contributions, which the verifier reconstructs from the VK's public wire encodings and the $C$ element respectively. The $\alpha$, $\beta$ cross-terms (present in $C$ via $K_i = \beta u_i + \alpha v_i + w_i$) ensure the prover must have committed to **the same witness** in $A$ and $B$.
 
With blinding ($r, s \neq 0$), the extra $[\delta]$ terms cancel across the pairing equation, leaving the same check.

## Common Soundness Attacks (Prover-Side)
 
Understanding what could go wrong illuminates why the formula is designed this way:
 
### 1. Forging $C$ with wrong witness
A cheating prover cannot compute the private witness commitment in $C$ without valid $w_i$ scalars, because $\left[\frac{\beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$ are fixed PK elements — the prover can only take their linear combination, and the coefficients must match those used in $A$ and $B$. This is enforced by the $\alpha$–$\beta$ coupling: forging $C$ independently would require knowing $\alpha$ and $\beta$, which is the discrete log problem.
 
### 2. Malleability attacks
A naive observer might try to modify $(A, B, C)$ to $(k \cdot A, k^{-1} \cdot B, C)$ — the pairing $e(kA, k^{-1}B) = e(A,B)$ is unchanged. But $C$ is tied to $A$ via the $sA$ term; perturbing $A$ without adjusting $C$ breaks the $e(C, \delta)$ check. The blinding factors $r, s$ ensure $C$ cannot be decoupled from $A, B$.
 
### 3. Simulation extractability
Because of the $\alpha$–$\beta$ cross-terms and the $\gamma$/$\delta$ separation, Groth16 achieves **simulation extractability** in the Generic Group Model with the ROM — meaning even if the adversary sees other valid proofs, they cannot forge a new one without a valid witness. This is a stronger property than plain knowledge soundness.

## Implementation Notes (ark-groth16)
 
In `ark-groth16`, the prover is in `src/prover.rs`:
 
```rust
// Sketch of key steps
pub fn create_proof<E, C, R>(
    circuit: C,
    pk: &ProvingKey<E>,
    rng: &mut R,
) -> Result<Proof<E>, SynthesisError>
{
    // 1. Generate witness via constraint synthesis
    let cs = ConstraintSystem::new_ref();
    circuit.generate_constraints(cs.clone())?;
    let witness = cs.witness_assignment();
 
    // 2. Compute h(x) via FFT
    let h = compute_h(&witness, &pk.vk.delta_g1, ...);
 
    // 3. Sample blinding factors
    let r = E::Fr::rand(rng);
    let s = E::Fr::rand(rng);
 
    // 4. MSMs for A, B, C
    let g1_a = pk.alpha_g1 + msm(&pk.a_query, &witness) + pk.delta_g1 * r;
    let g2_b = pk.beta_g2 + msm_g2(&pk.b_g2_query, &witness) + pk.delta_g2 * s;
    let g1_b = pk.beta_g1 + msm(&pk.b_g1_query, &witness) + pk.delta_g1 * s;
 
    let c = msm(&pk.l_query, &witness[l+1..])  // private wires
          + msm(&pk.h_query, &h)                // quotient poly
          + g1_a * s + g1_b * r - pk.delta_g1 * (r * s);
 
    Ok(Proof { a: g1_a, b: g2_b, c })
}
```
 
Key PK query arrays:
- `a_query`: $[u_i(\tau)]_1$ for all wires
- `b_g2_query` / `b_g1_query`: $[v_i(\tau)]_2$ and $[v_i(\tau)]_1$ for all wires
- `l_query`: $\left[\frac{\beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$ for **private** wires $i > \ell$
- `h_query`: $\left[\frac{\tau^i \cdot t(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$ for $i = 0,\ldots,n-2$

## Summary
 
```
Prover Pipeline
│
├── [Input]  Full witness w = (1, pub_1..pub_ℓ, priv_ℓ+1..priv_m)
│
├── [Step 1] Verify R1CS satisfaction — all n constraints hold
│
├── [Step 2] Compute QAP polynomials U(x), V(x), W(x) via IFFT
│
├── [Step 3] Compute quotient h(x) = (U·V - W) / t   via FFT division
│
├── [Step 4] Sample blinding scalars r, s ←$ 𝔽_r
│
├── [Step 5] A = [α]₁ + Σ wᵢ·[uᵢ(τ)]₁ + r·[δ]₁          (𝔾₁ MSM)
│
├── [Step 6] B = [β]₂ + Σ wᵢ·[vᵢ(τ)]₂ + s·[δ]₂          (𝔾₂ MSM)
│
├── [Step 7] C = Σ_priv wᵢ·Kᵢ/δ + Σ hᵢ·τⁱt(τ)/δ          (𝔾₁ MSM)
│              + s·A + r·B₁ - r·s·[δ]₁
│
└── [Output] Proof π = (A, B, C) ∈ 𝔾₁ × 𝔾₂ × 𝔾₁
```
 
The final proof is just **3 group elements** (~128–192 bytes for BN254), regardless of circuit size.

# Groth16 Verification: Checking the Proof
Given a proof $\pi = (A, B, C)$, a Verification Key (VK), and the public inputs $\mathbf{x} = (w_1, \ldots, w_\ell)$, the Groth16 verifier checks a **single pairing equation** that simultaneously verifies:
 
1. The prover knows a witness satisfying all R1CS constraints.
2. The private witness is consistent with what was committed to in $A$ and $B$.
3. The QAP division $U \cdot V - W = h \cdot t$ holds at $\tau$.
4. The public inputs match those used in the proof.
 
The entire check reduces to **3 pairing evaluations** and **1 multi-scalar multiplication (MSM)**, making Groth16 the most verifier-efficient general-purpose zk-SNARK in production.

# Step 1: Reconstruct the Public Input Accumulator
 
Before evaluating any pairing, the verifier computes a single $\mathbb{G}_1$ element that encodes all public inputs:
 
$$P_{\text{pub}} = K_0^{\text{pub}} + \sum_{i=1}^{\ell} w_i \cdot K_i^{\text{pub}} \in \mathbb{G}_1$$
 
Expanding using the definition of $K_i^{\text{pub}}$:
 
$$P_{\text{pub}} = \left[\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} w_i \cdot (\beta u_i(\tau) + \alpha v_i(\tau) + w_i(\tau))}{\gamma}\right]_1$$
 
This is a **Multi-Scalar Multiplication (MSM)** of $\ell + 1$ group elements with scalars $(1, w_1, \ldots, w_\ell)$.
 
> This is the **only variable-cost step** in verification — it scales with the number of public inputs $\ell$. A circuit with fewer public outputs is cheaper to verify.
 
### Why $\gamma$?
 
The division by $\gamma$ (instead of $\delta$ used for private wires) is the mechanism that **separates public and private wire commitments**. The verifier can reconstruct the public portion ($P_{\text{pub}}$) using only the VK and public inputs. The private portion was folded into $C$ by the prover under $\delta$. Without this separation, a cheating prover could mix public and private encodings to forge proofs.
 
---
 
## Step 2: The Pairing Check
 
The verifier accepts the proof if and only if:
 
$$\boxed{e(A,\ B) = e\!\left([\alpha]_1,\ [\beta]_2\right) \cdot e\!\left(P_{\text{pub}},\ [\gamma]_2\right) \cdot e\!\left(C,\ [\delta]_2\right)}$$
 
Equivalently (and more efficiently, by moving everything to one side):
 
$$e(A, B) \cdot e\!\left(-[\alpha]_1, [\beta]_2\right) \cdot e\!\left(-P_{\text{pub}}, [\gamma]_2\right) \cdot e\!\left(-C, [\delta]_2\right) = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{G}_T}$$
 
This is a **product-of-pairings** check that evaluates to the identity element $\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{G}_T}$ iff the equation holds. The negation trick allows all four pairings to be batched into a single **Miller loop** (used in all production implementations).
 
---
 
## Unpacking the Pairing Equation
 
To understand *why* this equation works, substitute the honest prover's $A$, $B$, $C$ (with $r = s = 0$ for clarity):
 
$$A = [\alpha + U(\tau)]_1, \quad B = [\beta + V(\tau)]_2, \quad C = \left[\frac{W_{\text{priv}}(\tau) + h(\tau)t(\tau)}{\delta}\right]_1$$
 
where $W_{\text{priv}}(\tau) = \sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m} w_i K_i$ (private wire cross-products).
 
**Left side:**
 
$$e(A, B) = e\!\left([\alpha + U(\tau)]_1,\ [\beta + V(\tau)]_2\right)$$
 
By bilinearity of $e$:
 
$$= e([\alpha]_1, [\beta]_2) \cdot e([\alpha]_1, [V(\tau)]_2) \cdot e([U(\tau)]_1, [\beta]_2) \cdot e([U(\tau)]_1, [V(\tau)]_2)$$
 
**QAP satisfaction substitution:**
 
Since the witness is valid: $U(\tau) \cdot V(\tau) = W(\tau) + h(\tau) \cdot t(\tau)$
 
$$e([U(\tau)]_1, [V(\tau)]_2) = e\!\left([W(\tau) + h(\tau)t(\tau)]_1, [1]_2\right)$$
 
**Splitting $W(\tau)$ into public and private parts:**
 
$$W(\tau) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} w_i w_i(\tau)}_{W_{\text{pub}}(\tau)} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{m} w_i w_i(\tau)}_{W_{\text{priv}}(\tau)}$$
 
**Assembling all cross-product terms:**
 
$$e([\alpha]_1, [V(\tau)]_2) \cdot e([U(\tau)]_1, [\beta]_2) \cdot e([W(\tau)]_1, [1]_2)$$
$$= e\!\left([\beta U(\tau) + \alpha V(\tau) + W(\tau)]_1, [1]_2\right)$$
 
Split this into public ($i \leq \ell$, divided by $\gamma$) and private ($i > \ell$, divided by $\delta$):
 
$$= e\!\left(\left[\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell} w_i(\beta u_i + \alpha v_i + w_i)}{\gamma}\right]_1 \cdot \gamma,\ [1]_2\right) \cdot e\!\left(\left[\frac{\sum_{i > \ell} w_i(\beta u_i + \alpha v_i + w_i)}{\delta}\right]_1 \cdot \delta,\ [1]_2\right)$$
 
The $\gamma$ and $\delta$ factors come from pairing with $[\gamma]_2$ and $[\delta]_2$ respectively:
 
$$= e\!\left(P_{\text{pub}},\ [\gamma]_2\right) \cdot e(C,\ [\delta]_2)$$
 
(where $C$ also absorbs the $h(\tau)t(\tau)/\delta$ term).
 
This exactly matches the right-hand side of the verification equation — **QED**.
 
---
 
## Effect of the Blinding Factors ($r, s \neq 0$)
 
With the ZK blinding terms present, $A$ and $B$ include $r[\delta]_1$ and $s[\delta]_2$. The cross terms produced in $e(A, B)$ include:
 
$$e(r[\delta]_1, [\beta + V(\tau)]_2) \cdot e([\alpha + U(\tau)]_1, s[\delta]_2) \cdot e(r[\delta]_1, s[\delta]_2)$$
 
These expand to extra $e([\delta]_1, [\delta]_2)$ factors. The prover's formula for $C$ includes $sA + rB_1 - rs[\delta]_1$, which contributes to $e(C, [\delta]_2)$:
 
$$e(sA + rB_1 - rs[\delta]_1,\ [\delta]_2)$$
 
It can be verified algebraically that all extra $e([\delta], [\delta])_T$ terms cancel exactly between the left side and the $e(C, [\delta]_2)$ term. The blinding is a **perfect masking** — it leaves the verification equation unchanged while making $A$, $B$, $C$ individually uniformly random from the verifier's perspective.
 
---
 
## What Each Pairing Term Enforces
 
| Pairing Term | What It Enforces |
|---|---|
| $e(A, B)$ | The prover committed to some linear combination of QAP polynomials in both $\mathbb{G}_1$ and $\mathbb{G}_2$ |
| $e([\alpha]_1, [\beta]_2)$ | The $\alpha, \beta$ "binding" — ensures $A$ and $B$ were formed using the PK, not arbitrary group elements |
| $e(P_{\text{pub}}, [\gamma]_2)$ | The public inputs are correctly included with the $\gamma$-separated VK encodings |
| $e(C, [\delta]_2)$ | The private witness satisfies the QAP; the quotient polynomial $h(x)$ is correctly embedded |
 
If the prover omits $[\alpha]_1$ from $A$ or $[\beta]_2$ from $B$, the $e([\alpha]_1, [\beta]_2)$ term on the right would not cancel. If the prover uses a wrong private witness, the $e(C, [\delta]_2)$ term would mismatch. If the public inputs are tampered with, $P_{\text{pub}}$ would be wrong.

## On-Chain Verification (Ethereum / EVM)
 
Groth16 is the predominant proof system for on-chain verification because the verifier's work is constant-time regardless of circuit complexity.
 
The EVM exposes a **BN254 pairing precompile** at address `0x08` (EIP-197). A typical Solidity verifier looks like:
 
```solidity
function verify(
    uint[2] memory a,       // A in G1
    uint[2][2] memory b,    // B in G2
    uint[2] memory c,       // C in G1
    uint[] memory publicInputs
) public view returns (bool) {
    // Step 1: Reconstruct P_pub
    Pairing.G1Point memory vk_x = vk.IC[0];
    for (uint i = 0; i < publicInputs.length; i++) {
        vk_x = Pairing.addition(vk_x, Pairing.scalar_mul(vk.IC[i+1], publicInputs[i]));
    }
 
    // Step 2: Check pairing equation
    // e(A, B) · e(-alpha, beta) · e(-vk_x, gamma) · e(-C, delta) == 1
    return Pairing.pairing(
        [a, vk.alpha, vk_x, c],
        [b, vk.beta,  vk.gamma, vk.delta]
    );
}
```
 
`vk.IC` is the array of $K_i^{\text{pub}}$ elements. The `Pairing.pairing` call invokes the precompile with all four pairs batched — a single call that returns true iff the product of pairings is $\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{G}_T}$.
 
**Gas cost on Ethereum (EIP-197):**
 
| Operation | Gas |
|---|---|
| Base pairing call | 45,000 |
| Per pairing pair | 34,000 |
| 4-pair Groth16 check | $45000 + 4 \times 34000 = 181{,}000$ gas |
| Public input MSM (per input) | ~$7{,}500$ gas (ecMul + ecAdd) |
 
For a circuit with 10 public inputs, total verification is roughly $\sim 250{,}000$ gas — under a standard Ethereum transaction's gas limit and significantly cheaper than re-executing the computation on-chain.

## Verification Failures and Their Meaning
 
| Failure mode | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| $e(A, B) \neq \text{RHS}$ (all terms) | Completely invalid proof — forged or corrupted |
| $P_{\text{pub}}$ reconstruction mismatch | Public inputs don't match those used to generate proof |
| $e(C, [\delta]_2)$ mismatch | Private witness invalid or QAP not satisfied |
| $e([\alpha], [\beta])$ mismatch | PK/VK mismatch — proof generated with different setup |
| Proof elements not on curve | Malformed proof — must be validated before pairing |
 
### Point Validation (Critical Security Check)
 
Before running the pairing equation, the verifier **must** check that $A \in \mathbb{G}_1$, $B \in \mathbb{G}_2$, $C \in \mathbb{G}_1$ are valid curve points and are **not the point at infinity**. Accepting the point at infinity as a valid group element allows trivial forgeries because $e(\mathcal{O}, \cdot) = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{G}_T}$ regardless of the other input, which can cause the pairing equation to pass vacuously.
 
In Solidity:
 
```solidity
require(a[0] != 0 || a[1] != 0, "A is point at infinity");
require(!Pairing.isInfinity(Pairing.G2Point(b)), "B is point at infinity");
```
 
In `ark-groth16`:
```rust
if proof.a.is_zero() || proof.b.is_zero() || proof.c.is_zero() {
    return Err(SynthesisError::UnexpectedIdentity);
}
```
 
**Subgroup membership checks** are also required on some curves (e.g., BLS12-381 has a cofactor $> 1$ in $\mathbb{G}_2$) to ensure the points are actually in the prime-order subgroup, not just on the curve. BN254's $\mathbb{G}_2$ has cofactor 1 so this is not required there, but **always verify for your specific curve**.

## Verifier Optimisation: Precomputing the $\alpha\beta$ Pairing
 
Since $e([\alpha]_1, [\beta]_2)$ depends only on the VK (not the proof), it is a **constant** that can be precomputed once and stored:
 
$$e_{\alpha\beta} = e([\alpha]_1, [\beta]_2) \in \mathbb{G}_T$$
 
The verification equation becomes:
 
$$e(A, B) = e_{\alpha\beta} \cdot e(P_{\text{pub}}, [\gamma]_2) \cdot e(C, [\delta]_2)$$
 
This reduces the online pairing count from 4 to 3. In `ark-groth16`:
 
```rust
pub struct PreparedVerifyingKey<E: Pairing> {
    pub vk: VerifyingKey<E>,
    pub alpha_g1_beta_g2: E::TargetField,  // precomputed e(α, β) ∈ G_T
    pub gamma_g2_neg_pc: E::G2Prepared,    // -[γ]₂ in Miller loop form
    pub delta_g2_neg_pc: E::G2Prepared,    // -[δ]₂ in Miller loop form
}
```
 
The `PreparedVerifyingKey` pre-processes $[\gamma]_2$ and $[\delta]_2$ into **Miller loop tables** — precomputed line evaluation coefficients that make the pairing significantly faster at proof time.

## Batch Verification
 
When verifying $k$ proofs $(\pi^{(1)}, \ldots, \pi^{(k)})$ for the **same circuit**, the verifier can batch them with random scalars $\rho_1, \ldots, \rho_k \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{F}_r$:
 
$$e\!\left(\sum_j \rho_j A^{(j)},\ B^{(1)}\right) \approx e_{\alpha\beta}^{\sum \rho_j} \cdot \prod_j e\!\left(\rho_j P_{\text{pub}}^{(j)}, [\gamma]_2\right) \cdot e\!\left(\sum_j \rho_j C^{(j)}, [\delta]_2\right)$$
 
This is **not exact batching** (unlike BLS signatures), but with a randomized linear combination, the probability of a false positive is $\leq k / |\mathbb{F}_r|$ — negligible for the $\sim 254$-bit fields used. Full batch verification reduces the number of $\mathbb{G}_2$ pairings from $3k$ to $3$, giving near-linear speedup for large batches.

## Comparison: Groth16 vs Other SNARKs (Verifier Perspective)
 
| Scheme | Proof size | Verifier ops | Setup |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Groth16** | 3 group elements ($\sim$128B on BN254) | 3 pairings + 1 MSM | Trusted, circuit-specific |
| PLONK | ~9 group elements + scalars | 2 pairings + MSMs | Trusted, universal |
| STARKs | ~50–200KB | Hash evaluations | Transparent |
| Halo2 | ~$\log n$ group elements | Multi-MSM | Transparent |
 
Groth16 has the **smallest proof size** and **fewest verifier operations** of any production zk-SNARK. This is the reason it dominates on-chain applications (Zcash Sapling, Tornado Cash, Hermez) where gas cost is a hard constraint. The price is a circuit-specific trusted setup.
